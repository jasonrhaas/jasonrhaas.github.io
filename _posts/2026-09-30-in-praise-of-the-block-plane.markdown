Sometimes a simple hand tool can outperform even the fanciest of power tools.

## It's just a plane, right?

Take my Lie-Nielsen block plane. It's just a plane. A metal blade in a
housing, used to shave wood. How different could one be from another?

Turns out, very. There's a lot that goes into making a simple plane a great
tool to work with.

I'd used hand planes before — basic block planes from Lowe's or Home Depot.
They mostly got the job done. The Lie-Nielsen does it with style and class.
It's the epitome of "they don't make 'em like they used to," except they do,
in Maine: cast iron, bronze, and an A2 hardened steel blade. The components
make all the difference, and you feel it the moment you use it.

## How I got here

A few years ago I wouldn't have believed a hand tool could beat a power tool at
anything. When I first got into woodworking, power tools felt like the obvious
answer to everything. Faster, more accurate, less skill required. Hand tools
were for the old-school masters.

I never learned woodworking properly. The closest I've gotten to formal
training is a weekend class at Woodcraft. Other than that, Steve Ramsey on
YouTube taught me the fundamentals: safety, technique, repeatability.

Steve's whole approach is to keep it simple and get results, and I think
that's 100% the right way to go for beginners. You don't need a
cabinet-maker's shop to build a nice bookshelf. You need a few tools, some
patience, and a willingness to mess up a board or two.

I suspect even experienced craftsmen eventually hit a point where they realize
simple is more elegant than complex. I'm starting to feel that way myself.

## From "just enough tool" to a full shop

I caught the woodworking bug during Covid, and I've spent some money on tools
since then. But I've always been pretty budget conscious about it. My rule was
to never buy more tool than I needed for the job in front of me.

Somehow, one job at a time, that rule still added up to what almost looks like
a full wood shop: table saw, router table, planer, jointer, track saw, the
works.

One thing I've learned along the way: with tools, you mostly get what you pay
for. There are exceptions — some of the more extravagant stuff from
Woodpeckers or Laguna is priced like jewelry — but in general, spending more
gets you a better tool. The block plane is proof.

## Sharpening was the unlock

Hand tools are intimidating. They evoke the skill of the old master
woodworkers, and a dull plane in a beginner's hands is a frustrating thing.
It took me a little while to figure out how to sharpen properly.

Once I did, it was a huge unlock. And on a good tool, sharpening is quick and
simple, and the edge stays sharp for a good long while. That changes the whole
relationship with the tool. It stops being a chore to maintain and becomes
something you just pick up and use.

## A connection to the wood

The block plane is a joy to use. It's like having a direct connection to the
wood. You can feel the grain through the tool, and you can dial in the cut
until the shavings come off thin enough to see light through them.

It seems like a simple tool, but it has incredible power and versatility:
chamfering an edge, easing a corner, trimming end grain, sneaking up on a
perfect fit.

Power tools get the job done quickly, accurately, and precisely — especially
if you pay up for the high-end stuff like Festool. But the tradeoff is real.
You lose that connection to the wood, there's dust everywhere, and your shop
fills with the scream of a spinning blade.

## I'm not in a hurry (when woodworking)

So these days, if I have the option to use a hand tool, I'll take it over a
power tool, even if it takes me a little longer.

I'm not doing this to make money. I'm doing it for the fun of it. And a quiet
shop, a sharp plane, and a pile of translucent shavings on the bench is about
as much fun as woodworking gets.
